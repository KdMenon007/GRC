
## 1. Overview

**Information Security Objectives (IS Objectives)** define what the organization wants to achieve through its Information Security Management System (ISMS).

The ISMS as a whole is intended to protect the required:

- **Confidentiality**
    
- **Integrity**
    
- **Availability**
    

of information and the business processes that depend on that information.

The important principle is:

> **Information security objectives should be derived from business objectives and translated into measurable security outcomes.**

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
Business Objectives
        ↓
IT Objectives
        ↓
Information Security Objectives
        ↓
Risk Assessment & Security Requirements
        ↓
Security Measures / Projects
        ↓
Measurement & Monitoring
        ↓
Continual Improvement
```

---

# 2. Relationship Between Business Objectives and IS Objectives

Information security should not operate independently from the business.

The organization's **business objectives** provide the foundation for defining IT objectives and information security objectives.

### Example

### Business Objective

> Provide reliable online banking services 24/7.

### IT Objective

> Maintain high availability and resilience of banking infrastructure.

### Information Security Objective

> Maintain availability of critical banking services at the required level and minimize security-related service disruptions.

### Measures

- High-availability infrastructure
    
- Disaster recovery
    
- Backup
    
- Security monitoring
    
- Incident response
    

Therefore, security objectives should directly contribute to business success.

---

# 3. Why IS Objectives Are Important

Failure to achieve an information security objective can directly affect business objectives.

### Example

Business objective:

> Process customer transactions without interruption.

IS objective:

> Maintain availability of critical transaction systems.

If the IS objective is not achieved and a ransomware attack causes prolonged system downtime, the business objective may also fail.

Therefore:

```text
Failure of IS Objective
          ↓
Security / Operational Impact
          ↓
Business Impact
          ↓
Possible Financial / Reputational Loss
```

---

# 4. IS Objectives and the CIA Triad

IS objectives should be consistent with the fundamental information security protection objectives.

## Confidentiality

Ensuring information is accessible only to authorized individuals.

### Example Objective

> Reduce unauthorized access to sensitive customer information.

---

## Integrity

Ensuring information remains accurate, complete, and protected from unauthorized modification.

### Example Objective

> Ensure critical financial records are protected against unauthorized modification.

---

## Availability

Ensuring information and systems are available when required.

### Example Objective

> Maintain availability of critical business applications according to defined business requirements.

---

# 5. Alignment with the Information Security Policy

IS objectives should be aligned with the organization's **Information Security Policy**.

The relationship is:

```text
Business Strategy
       ↓
Information Security Policy
       ↓
Information Security Objectives
       ↓
Risk Treatment
       ↓
Controls and Activities
```

The policy provides the overall direction, while the objectives translate that direction into specific results that the organization wants to achieve.

---

# 6. Alignment with Business Goals

IS objectives should always be aligned with the organization's overall goals.

They should not be created simply because a security team wants to implement a particular technology.

### Poor approach

> "Deploy a new SIEM."

This is primarily a **measure or implementation activity**, not an information security objective.

### Better objective

> "Improve detection and response capability for security incidents affecting critical systems."

### Possible measure

> Implement and configure a SIEM platform.

This distinction is important.

---

# 7. Objective vs. Measure

One of the most important concepts when defining IS objectives is to distinguish between the **objective** and the **measure used to achieve it**.

|Information Security Objective|Measure|
|---|---|
|Improve detection of security incidents|Implement SIEM|
|Reduce unauthorized access|Implement MFA|
|Improve vulnerability management|Deploy vulnerability scanner|
|Protect sensitive information|Implement encryption|
|Improve employee security awareness|Conduct awareness training|

The **objective describes the desired outcome**.

The **measure describes what the organization does to achieve the outcome**.

---

# 8. Objectives Should Be Long-Term and Meaningful

IS objectives should describe genuine, meaningful, and preferably longer-term goals.

They should not simply list operational tasks.

### Incorrect

> Install EDR on all endpoints.

This describes an implementation activity.

### Better

> Improve endpoint threat detection and response capability across the organization.

The EDR deployment can then be one of the activities used to achieve the objective.

---

# 9. Risk Assessment as a Basis for IS Objectives

Security requirements and the results of risk assessments provide another important basis for defining IS objectives.

The process can be represented as:

```text
Identify Business Requirements
           ↓
Identify Security Requirements
           ↓
Perform Risk Assessment
           ↓
Identify Significant Risks
           ↓
Define IS Objectives
           ↓
Define Risk Treatment
           ↓
Implement Controls
```

### Example

Risk assessment identifies:

> High risk of ransomware affecting critical servers.

Possible IS objective:

> Reduce the likelihood and business impact of ransomware affecting critical systems.

Possible measures:

- EDR
    
- Network segmentation
    
- Backup
    
- Patch management
    
- Security awareness
    
- Incident response exercises
    

---

# 10. Planning How Objectives Will Be Achieved

Defining an objective alone is not sufficient.

The organization should determine **how the objective will be achieved**.

Planning should identify:

- Activities
    
- Required resources
    
- Responsible persons
    
- Required capabilities
    
- Timeline
    
- Measurement method
    
- Evaluation process
    

### Example

**Objective:**

> Reduce critical vulnerabilities in production systems.

**Activities:**

- Conduct vulnerability scanning
    
- Prioritize critical vulnerabilities
    
- Patch vulnerable systems
    
- Perform remediation verification
    

**Responsible:**

> Infrastructure Security Team

**Timeline:**

> Six months

**Measurement:**

> Percentage of critical vulnerabilities remediated within the defined SLA

---

# 11. Resources and Responsibilities

When planning an IS objective, the organization should identify the resources required.

Resources can include:

### People

- Security analysts
    
- System administrators
    
- Security engineers
    
- Risk managers
    

### Technology

- SIEM
    
- EDR
    
- Vulnerability management tools
    
- Identity management systems
    

### Financial Resources

- Security software
    
- Training
    
- Consulting
    
- Security assessments
    

### Time

The organization should define realistic implementation timelines.

---

# 12. Security Requirements Should Be Considered Early

Security requirements should be incorporated **from the beginning** of projects, products, and systems.

Security should not be treated as something added after implementation.

### Example

When developing a new customer application:

```text
Business Requirements
        ↓
Security Requirements
        ↓
System Design
        ↓
Development
        ↓
Security Testing
        ↓
Deployment
        ↓
Monitoring
```

Security requirements should be considered throughout the lifecycle.

---

# 13. Security as a Non-Functional Requirement

Information security requirements are often **non-functional requirements**.

Examples:

- Authentication
    
- Availability
    
- Encryption
    
- Logging
    
- Access control
    
- Auditability
    
- Data protection
    
- Resilience
    

For example:

### Functional Requirement

> The application must allow customers to transfer money.

### Security / Non-Functional Requirement

> Customer transactions must be authenticated and protected against unauthorized modification.

Both should be considered during system planning.

---

# 14. Employee Awareness and IS Objectives

Security objectives may also require employee training and awareness.

For example:

### Objective

> Reduce the likelihood of successful phishing attacks.

### Supporting activities

- Security awareness training
    
- Phishing simulations
    
- Email security controls
    
- Reporting mechanisms
    
- Targeted training for high-risk users
    

This demonstrates that IS objectives can require both **technical and organizational measures**.

---

# 15. SMART IS Objectives

The implementation guide recommends using **SMART** objectives where appropriate.

SMART stands for:

|Letter|Meaning|Example|
|---|---|---|
|**S**|Specific|Reduce critical vulnerabilities|
|**M**|Measurable|By 80%|
|**A**|Achievable|Based on available resources|
|**R**|Relevant|Supports business security requirements|
|**T**|Time-bound|Within six months|

### Complete Example

> **Reduce critical vulnerabilities in production systems by 80% within six months.**

This is much stronger than:

> "Improve vulnerability management."

---

# 16. Measuring Achievement of IS Objectives

The organization should be able to determine whether its objectives are being achieved.

Measurement can be performed using:

- KPIs
    
- Metrics
    
- Security dashboards
    
- Audit results
    
- Risk indicators
    
- Performance reports
    

### Example

Objective:

> Improve incident response.

Possible KPI:

> Percentage of high-severity incidents contained within 60 minutes.

Target:

> 95%.

---

# 17. Examples of IS Objectives and KPIs

|IS Objective|Possible KPI|
|---|---|
|Reduce vulnerabilities|% critical vulnerabilities remediated within SLA|
|Improve incident response|% high-severity incidents contained within target time|
|Improve security awareness|% employees completing training|
|Reduce unauthorized access|Number of unauthorized access incidents|
|Improve availability|Security-related downtime|
|Improve backup resilience|% critical systems successfully backed up|
|Improve detection|Mean Time to Detect (MTTD)|
|Improve response|Mean Time to Respond (MTTR)|

---

# 18. "If Practicable" and Measurement

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 requires objectives to be measurable **where practicable**.

This means the organization should consider whether meaningful measurement can realistically be implemented.

It does **not** mean that an organization should simply avoid measuring objectives.

The organization should determine:

- What should be measured
    
- How it can be measured
    
- Whether measurement provides useful information
    
- Whether the required resources are reasonable
    

In practice, measurable objectives are strongly preferred because they allow management to determine whether the ISMS is achieving its intended results.

---

# 19. Avoid Too Many Objectives

Defining a very large number of objectives can create unnecessary complexity.

Especially during the initial ISMS implementation, it is often better to define a **small number of meaningful and balanced objectives**.

The organization should consider:

```text
Business Value
      +
Security Importance
      +
Risk Reduction
      +
Implementation Effort
```

The objective should provide meaningful value without creating an excessive measurement burden.

---

# 20. Review and Update IS Objectives

IS objectives should not remain unchanged forever.

They should be periodically reviewed to determine whether they remain:

- Relevant
    
- Appropriate
    
- Achievable
    
- Aligned with business objectives
    
- Consistent with the organization's risk profile
    

Objectives may need to change because of:

- New business strategies
    
- New technologies
    
- New threats
    
- Security incidents
    
- Regulatory changes
    
- Changes in risk
    
- Organizational changes
    

---

# 21. Example: Changing Business Requirements

Suppose a company moves from on-premises infrastructure to cloud services.

### Previous Business Objective

> Maintain secure internal data center operations.

### New Business Objective

> Provide scalable cloud-based services globally.

The IS objectives may therefore need to change to include:

- Cloud security
    
- Identity and access management
    
- Third-party security
    
- Data protection
    
- Cloud monitoring
    

This demonstrates why objectives must be reviewed regularly.

# 23. Documentation Requirements

Under **ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Clause 6.2**, the organization must maintain documented information about its information security objectives.

The objectives should be appropriately documented and maintained.

IS objectives are commonly included in:

- Information Security Policy
    
- Information Security Strategy
    
- ISMS objectives register
    
- Security management plan

# 26. Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Defining a Technology as an Objective

❌

> Implement Microsoft Sentinel.

This is a project/activity.

✅

> Improve security monitoring and threat detection capabilities.

---

### Mistake 2: Creating Vague Objectives

❌

> Improve cybersecurity.

There is no clear target or measurement.

✅

> Reduce critical security vulnerabilities by 80% within six months.

---

### Mistake 3: Objectives Not Linked to Business

❌

> Deploy a new security tool because competitors use it.

✅

> Improve protection of critical customer-facing services based on identified business and security risks.

---

### Mistake 4: Too Many Objectives

Creating dozens of objectives can make monitoring difficult.

Better:

> Focus initially on a small number of meaningful objectives that address significant business and security requirements.

---

### Mistake 5: No Measurement

An objective should have a meaningful way to determine whether it has been achieved, where practicable.

# 27. Relationship with Risk Management

IS objectives and risk management are closely connected.

```text
Business Requirements
        ↓
Security Requirements
        ↓
Risk Assessment
        ↓
Significant Risks
        ↓
IS Objectives
        ↓
Risk Treatment
        ↓
Controls
        ↓
KPIs / Measurement
        ↓
Management Review
```

For example:

**Risk:** Ransomware affecting critical systems.

**IS Objective:** Reduce ransomware likelihood and impact.

**Controls/Measures:**

- EDR
    
- Backup
    
- Network segmentation
    
- Patch management
    
- Awareness training
    
- Incident response
    

**KPI:**

> Percentage of critical systems with tested recovery capability.

---

# 28. Management's Role in IS Objectives

Management should ensure that IS objectives:

- Support business objectives
    
- Are consistent with the security policy
    
- Reflect significant risks
    
- Have appropriate resources
    
- Have assigned responsibilities
    
- Are monitored
    
- Are reviewed periodically
    

Management should also use objective results during **management reviews** to determine whether the ISMS is achieving its intended outcomes.

---

# 30. Easy Memory Formula

Remember **IS Objectives** as:

> **B-R-S-M-R**

**B – Business Alignment**  
Objectives must support business goals.

**R – Risk-Based**  
Use security requirements and risk assessment results.

**S – SMART**  
Make objectives specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound where appropriate.

**M – Measurement**  
Use KPIs and metrics to evaluate achievement where practicable.

**R – Review**  
Regularly review and update objectives.

---

# Final Summary

**ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Clause 6.2** requires organizations to establish appropriate information security objectives.

The key principle is:

> **IS objectives should translate business and security requirements into measurable security outcomes.**

A strong IS objective should:

1. Align with **business objectives**.
    
2. Support **confidentiality, integrity, and availability**.
    
3. Consider **security requirements and risk assessment results**.
    
4. Be **meaningful and long-term**, rather than simply describing a technical activity.
    
5. Be **SMART where appropriate**.
    
6. Have defined **resources and responsibilities**.
    
7. Have a **timeframe**.
    
8. Be **measurable where practicable**.
    
9. Be monitored using **KPIs or other appropriate measures**.
    
10. Be **regularly reviewed and updated**.
    
11. Be supported by an **implementation plan**.
    
12. Be maintained as **documented information**.
    

### One-line interview answer

> **"ISO 27001 IS objectives are business-aligned, risk-informed, measurable security goals that define what the organization intends to achieve to protect its information and business processes, with defined responsibilities, resources, timelines, and methods for evaluating achievement."**
